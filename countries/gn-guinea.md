Source [1] also records that **more than 24 indigenous languages are spoken**, the largest being Susu,

**Official-language instrument:** `[NEEDS SOURCE]` — and this is now a priority, because the claim is
unusual enough to need its instrument.

**Languages Ubuntu Heritage does not have:** all nine, and the other fifteen-plus.

**This is the most important language finding in the folder.** Guinea has done, in law, what South
Africa did in 1996: **named a set of indigenous languages as official rather than merely tolerated**.
Ubuntu Heritage's language layer was built on the premise that this arrangement is what a decolonised
state looks like. Guinea is the West African proof that South Africa is not alone in it — and no
existing app data reflects that.

**Same structural blocker as Burkina Faso** — the app speaks none of these
([`bf-burkina-faso.md`](bf-burkina-faso.md#languages)) — but note the difference: for Algeria or
Morocco the missing languages have no official standing to lose. Here the app is failing to represent
**eight official African languages**.

## 🔴 N'Ko — an African script for African languages

| | |
|---|---|
| **Invented by** | **Solomana Kanté** (1922 – 23 November 1987), Guinean writer, neographer and educator [4] |
| **Year** | **1949**, after five years of experimenting with writing systems [4] |
| **For** | The **Manding** language varieties of Africa [4] |
| **The name means** | ***"I say"* — in all Manding languages** [4] |
| **Status in Guinea** | Listed among the country's languages by source [1] |

**Why this belongs at the centre of Ubuntu Heritage's argument, not in a footnote.**

The premise behind a great deal of colonial language policy was that African languages were oral, and
that to write them you had to borrow someone else's alphabet. **Kanté answered that by building one.**
Not a transliteration scheme and not a missionary orthography — an alphabet designed from scratch, by
a Guinean, for Manding, and named with the words *"I say"*.

This project is called *Reclaiming African Voices*. **N'Ko is a voice that reclaimed its own script**,
seven years before Ghana's independence and forty-seven before South Africa's Constitution named
eleven languages. Everything the app does with the eleven — treating a language as first-class,
endonym first, not an English label with a translation attached — is what Kanté did with a pen in
1949.

⚠️ Sourcing note: the N'Ko facts here come from source [4] (Wikipedia's Solomana Kanté and N'Ko script
entries, plus a Guinean biographical directory). **Before this becomes app content it deserves a
stronger, ideally Guinean or academic, source** — it is too important to rest on an encyclopaedia
summary. Logged as the file's top open question.

**Framing notes.**

- **Guinea said no, alone.** In the 1958 referendum every other French African territory chose the
  French Community; **Guinea voted for immediate independence** [1]. `[NEEDS SOURCE]` for "alone", but
  source [1]'s framing — a choice offered, and overwhelmingly refused — is already the point.
  Compare Côte d'Ivoire, which became an autonomous member of the same French Community in December
  1958 ([`ci-cote-d-ivoire.md`](ci-cote-d-ivoire.md)). **Two neighbours, the same offer, opposite
  answers, six weeks apart.** That contrast is worth building something around.
- **Samori Touré held the French off for twenty years** [1]. Put him beside Omar Mukhtar in Libya
  ([`ly-libya.md`](ly-libya.md)) and Kgosi Sechele at Dimawe in Botswana
  ([`bw-botswana.md`](bw-botswana.md)): armed African resistance to colonial conquest was
  continent-wide, sustained, and frequently effective for years.
- **Kirina and Mansa Musa belong to Mali's story too** — see [`ml-mali.md`](ml-mali.md). The Mali
  Empire covered several of these modern borders; the files should say so rather than each claiming it.

## Literature & voices

| Writer | Work | Year | Language | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Solomana Kanté** (1922–1987) | **the N'Ko alphabet** | **1949** | for Manding | [4] |
| Camara Laye (1928–1980) | ***L'Enfant noir*** (*The African Child* / *The Dark Child*) — autobiographical | **1953** | French | [4] |
| Camara Laye | *Le Regard du roi* (*The Radiance of the King*) | `[NEEDS year]` | French | [4] |
| Fodeba Keïta | composer of the national anthem | 1958 | — | [2] |

**Guinea gives this project both halves of its own argument, in the same decade.**

- **Camara Laye, 1953**: an African writing his own childhood in **French**, in a book source [4]
  calls among the earliest major works of Francophone African literature.
- **Solomana Kanté, 1949**: an African who declined the choice altogether and **built a script**.

Four years apart. Neither is the sell-out and neither is the purist — they are two answers to the same
question this whole app is organised around: *in what form does an African voice survive?* Plaatje
wrote *Mhudi* in English and compiled Setswana proverbs; Vilakazi wrote isiZulu into a university.
**Guinea's pair is the sharpest statement of that dilemma anywhere in the research so far**, and it
should go into the submission narrative.

## Heritage & sites

- **Mount Nimba Strict Nature Reserve** — inscribed **1982**, **Natural**, **shared with Côte
  d'Ivoire**, and **listed as in danger**. A "sky island" of local microclimates with rich endemic
  flora and fauna. Source [3], via [`ci-cote-d-ivoire.md`](ci-cote-d-ivoire.md); source [1] confirms
  "the Guinean and Ivorian sides of the Nimba Massif are a UNESCO Strict Nature Reserve".
