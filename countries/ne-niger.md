| Tamasheq · Fulfulde · Kanuri | Spoken — see the ethnic table | No | [1] |

**Official-language instrument:** **the 2025 transitional charter** [1] — source [1]'s exact wording is
"Hausa is the official and national language; English and French serve as the working languages as per
the 2025 transitional charter." `[NEEDS SOURCE]` for the charter's text and article.

**Languages Ubuntu Heritage does not have:** Hausa, Zarma/Songhay, Tamasheq, Fulfulde, Kanuri, French.

🔴 **An African language displaced French as official, and English was elevated alongside it.** That
second half matters: this is not a rejection of all foreign languages but a **re-ranking** — an African
language first, and the colonial language demoted to a tool. Read it beside:

- **Mali, June 2023** — French removed as official, thirteen national languages adopted
  ([`ml-mali.md`](ml-mali.md))
- **Niger, 2025** — Hausa official, French demoted to working language

⚠️ **And read it with its politics.** The charter comes from a **military government** installed by the
**26 July 2023 coup** [1], the same pattern as Mali. **Ubuntu Heritage must not present Sahelian
language decolonisation as an unambiguous good story while omitting who enacted it and how they took
power.** The project's rule against sanitising applies in both directions: neither dismiss the language
reform because of its authors, nor launder the authors because of the reform.

**Same structural blocker as Burkina Faso** — see [`bf-burkina-faso.md`](bf-burkina-faso.md#languages).

## 🔴 National anthem — replaced because a Frenchman wrote it

- **Title:** *L'Honneur de la Patrie* (French) — "The Honour of the Fatherland"
- **Adopted:** **22 June 2023**, replacing *La Nigérienne*
- **Words and music by:** a **committee** [2]
- **Recording we could use:** none sourced yet
- **Source:** [2]

> **Source [2], in full substance:** the Nigerien government began replacing *La Nigérienne* **in
> 2019**, **"citing concerns that it was composed by French citizens and contained 'racist' and
> subservient language."** The replacement, adopted in June 2023, **"emphasizes national pride and
> Niger's African identity."** A military coup followed within weeks — **and the anthem change was
> kept.**
>
> 🔴 **This is the clearest single act of cultural decolonisation documented anywhere in this folder.**
> A state examined the words it made schoolchildren sing, concluded they were racist and submissive
> and written by the coloniser, and replaced them. Compare:
>
> - **Morocco** kept the protectorate melody and rewrote the words ([`ma-morocco.md`](ma-morocco.md))
> - **The Gambia** kept a Mandinka melody under English words ([`gm-gambia.md`](gm-gambia.md))
> - **Niger** threw the whole thing out and said why
>
> Note the irony the file should keep rather than hide: **the new anthem is still in French** [2].
> Decolonisation in progress, not completed — which is a more honest thing to show a reader than a
> tidy victory.

**Framing notes.**

- **Kanem–Bornu lasted from the 8th to the 19th century** [1] — over a thousand years, and it barely
  appears in general histories. Put it beside the Alawi dynasty's four centuries in Morocco
  ([`ma-morocco.md`](ma-morocco.md)): African political continuity, at length, documented.
- **The empires here are shared.** Songhai and Mali run through [`ml-mali.md`](ml-mali.md) and
  [`gn-guinea.md`](gn-guinea.md); Kanem–Bornu runs into Chad and Nigeria. Write them once across the
  folder.
- **The ethnic figures are from the 2001 census** [1] — a quarter-century old. Label them.
- **W–Arly–Pendjari is shared with Benin and Burkina Faso** [3] and appears in all three files.

## Peoples & cultures

**2001 census** [1] — note the age of the data:

| Group | Share |
|---|---|
| **Hausa** | **55.4%** |
| Zarma and Songhay | 21.0% |
| **Tuareg** | 9.3% |
| Fulani | 8.5% |
| Kanuri | 4.7% |
| Gurma · Toubou · Arab | 0.4% each |

- **Hausa at 55.4%** [1] explains the 2025 language decision: this is not an elevation of a minority
  tongue but the recognition of what most people already speak. **Hausa is also one of the most widely
  spoken languages in Africa**, reaching deep into Nigeria `[NEEDS SOURCE]` — see
  [`ng-nigeria.md`](ng-nigeria.md).
- **The Tuareg (9.3%)** connect this file to Mali, Libya and Algeria — and to Ibrahim al-Koni, the
  Tuareg-raised novelist in [`ly-libya.md`](ly-libya.md). **Agadez**, the World Heritage city below,
  is a Tuareg centre `[NEEDS SOURCE]`.
- **Gurma appears in Burkina Faso's table too** ([`bf-burkina-faso.md`](bf-burkina-faso.md)) as
  Gourmantché.

## Heritage & sites

Three UNESCO World Heritage Sites [3]:

| Site | Inscribed | Type | Shared | In danger | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Aïr and Ténéré Natural Reserves** | 1991 | Natural | — | **Yes** | Parts of the Aïr Mountains, with isolated Sahelian flora and fauna |
| **W–Arly–Pendjari Complex** | 1996 | Natural | **Benin, Burkina Faso** | No | Sudano-Sahelian savanna, grasslands, wood savanna and shrubland; large mammals |
| **Historic Centre of Agadez** | 2013 | Cultural | — | No | A desert gateway of **mudbrick architecture**, with **the world's tallest mudbrick minaret** |

- **The world's tallest mudbrick minaret** [3] is a superlative worth using. It belongs to the same
  Sudano-Sahelian earthen tradition as Djenné and the Tomb of Askia in Mali
  ([`ml-mali.md`](ml-mali.md)), the mosques of northern Côte d'Ivoire
  ([`ci-cote-d-ivoire.md`](ci-cote-d-ivoire.md)) and the ksour of Mauritania
  ([`mr-mauritania.md`](mr-mauritania.md)). **That is now six files holding one architectural
  civilisation.**
