## National anthem

- **Title (own language):** *L'Aube Nouvelle* (French)
- **Title (English):** "The Dawn of a New Day"
- **Adopted:** 30 July 1960
- **Words and music by:** Gilbert Jean Dagnon (both) [2]
- **Recording we could use:** none sourced yet
- **Source:** [2]

> **The best small detail in the folder.** The anthem was adopted when the country was still
> **Dahomey**. When it was renamed **Benin** in 1975, the same composition was kept — which meant the
> lyrics had to be adapted to fit the country's new **two-syllable** name into a melodic space written
> for a **three-syllable** one [2].
>
> A nation renaming itself and having to re-cut its own anthem to make the new name fit is the most
> concrete illustration of decolonisation-as-work this research has turned up. Put it beside Burkina
> Faso, which built a new name out of three of its own languages
> ([`bf-burkina-faso.md`](bf-burkina-faso.md)), and Morocco, which kept a colonial-era melody and
> changed the words ([`ma-morocco.md`](ma-morocco.md)).

**Framing notes.**

- ⚠️ **Whydah was a major port of the transatlantic slave trade, and this file does not yet say so
  with a source.** Source [1] records Agaja's 1727 conquest of Whydah and Allada without stating what
  those ports were used for. The Kingdom of Dahomey's role in the trade — as a supplier, not only a
  victim — is documented history and it is the hardest thing in this file to tell. **It must not be
  omitted, and it must not be told carelessly.** Ubuntu Heritage already refuses the sanitised version
  of Botswana's *botlhanka* ([`bw-botswana.md`](bw-botswana.md)); this is the same obligation.
  `[NEEDS SOURCE]` — and it needs a good one.
- **1991 deserves emphasis.** A Marxist military ruler of eighteen years losing an election and
  leaving [1] is one of the cleaner democratic transitions on the continent, and the kind of fact that
  gets skipped when African political history is told only as coups.
- **The country has been renamed twice and changed system three times in twenty years** (1972, 1974,
  1975, 1990). Name and identity are unusually live here.

**Religion — 2013 census** [1]: Christianity 48.5% (Roman Catholic 25.5%, **Celestial Church of Christ
6.7%**, Methodist 3.4%, other 12.9%) · Islam 27.7% · **Vodun 11.6%** · other local traditional
religions 2.6% · other 2.6% · none 5.8%. A 2020 Pew estimate [1] gives Christianity 53%, Islam 31%,
other (including traditional) 11%, none 5%.

- ⚠️ **Vodun is a census religion here, not folklore.** **11.6% of Beninese declared Vodun in an
  official census** [1], and traditional religion adds 2.6% more. Anywhere Ubuntu Heritage touches
  this, it must be handled as a **living faith with adherents**, exactly as Christianity and Islam
  are — never as "voodoo", never as spectacle. This is the same rule the project applies to *Modimo*
  and *Badimo* in [`bw-botswana.md`](bw-botswana.md).
- **Benin is the origin point of Vodun's diaspora across the Atlantic** — `[NEEDS SOURCE]`, and it is
  the single most important claim this file could add. It connects Benin to Haiti, Brazil and the
  American South, and it makes this small country central to any account of African culture that
  survived the crossing.
- **The Celestial Church of Christ (6.7%)** is an African-founded church large enough to be counted
  separately in the census [1] — African Christianity as something authored here, not imported whole.
- **The Batammariba** and their *takienta* tower houses — see Heritage.

## Literature & voices

| Writer | Work | Year | Language | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Paul Hazoumé (1890–1980) | ***Doguicimi*** — set in the Kingdom of Dahomey under King Gézo (1818–58) | **1938** | French | [4] |
| Olympe Bhêly-Quénum (b. 1928) | *Le Chant du lac* · *Snares without End* | `[NEEDS years]` | French | [4] |
| Jean Pliya | named; explored tradition against modernity after independence | `[NEEDS SOURCE]` | French | [4] |

***Doguicimi* (1938) is the find here.** Source [4] describes Hazoumé — a writer, educator,
**ethnologist** and politician — as having "merged French language with traditional storytelling
techniques", in a novel set inside his own country's precolonial kingdom, published while that country
was still a French colony.

**The connection worth making, and it is a strong one.** *Mhudi* was written c. 1917 and published in
1930: an African novelist using the coloniser's language to narrate his own people's precolonial past,
on his own terms, under colonial rule. *Doguicimi* does the same thing in 1938 in Dahomey. **Plaatje
is not an isolated South African event — he is part of a continental movement**, and Hazoumé is the
West African evidence. If Ubuntu Heritage ever extends its four pillars beyond South Africa, this
pairing is the argument.

## Heritage & sites

Three UNESCO World Heritage Sites [3]:

| Site | Inscribed | Type | Shared | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Royal Palaces of Abomey** | 1985 | Cultural | — | Palaces surrounded by cob walls, with polychrome bas-reliefs, from the **Kingdom of Dahomey** |
| W–Arly–Pendjari Complex | 2017 | **Natural** | **Niger, Burkina Faso** | Sudano-Sahelian savanna; African elephants, lions, cheetahs |
| Koutammakou, the Land of the Batammariba | 2023 | Cultural | **Togo** | Landscape shaped by the Batammariba; mudbrick tower houses called *takienta* |

- **Abomey was danger-listed after tornado damage in 1984 and removed in 2007 after restoration** [3]
  — a third recovery story, alongside Ghadamès ([`ly-libya.md`](ly-libya.md)) and Ichkeul
  ([`tn-tunisia.md`](tn-tunisia.md)).
- **The bas-reliefs of Abomey are a written record in relief** — the Kingdom of Dahomey's own account
  of itself, on its own walls. For a project about voices that were not written down in European
  scripts, that is a direct and usable idea.
- **Two of the three sites are shared with neighbours** [3] — Niger and Burkina Faso; Togo. These files
  should cross-link rather than each claiming the site alone.
