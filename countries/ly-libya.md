**Official-language instrument:** **Constitutional Declaration, article 1** [1]. This is the first file
in the folder where the *instrument itself is named* rather than left as `[NEEDS SOURCE]` — the
standard the rest should reach.

**Languages Ubuntu Heritage does not have:** all of them.

⚠️ **A gap that matters more than it looks.** Source [1] does **not** mention any Berber/Amazigh
language, yet the same page records **5% of the population as Berber** [1]. Amazigh languages
(Nafusi, Tamasheq and others) are therefore almost certainly spoken in Libya and simply absent from
the source. **Do not add them from memory** — but do not read their absence here as evidence they are
not spoken. This is exactly the silence the "official ≠ spoken" trap in [README.md](README.md) warns
about, and under Gaddafi the suppression of Amazigh identity was policy. Flagged as an open question.

**Same blocker as Burkina Faso** — see [`bf-burkina-faso.md`](bf-burkina-faso.md#languages).

## National anthem

- **Title (own language):** ليبيا ليبيا ليبيا — *Libya, Libya, Libya*
- **Title (English):** "Libya, Libya, Libya"
- **Adopted:** first published **24 June 1955**; abolished 1969; **restored 2011**
- **Words by:** Al Bashir Al Arebi
- **Music by:** Mohamed Abdel Wahab
- **Recording we could use:** none sourced yet
- **Source:** [2]

> **This anthem has been deposed and restored, and it shows.** Source [2] records the whole arc: after
> independence a competition selected it, and it **debuted at King Idris's wedding in 1955**; Gaddafi's
> 1969 coup abolished the monarchy and replaced it; during the 2011 civil war **rebel forces took it
> up as their anthem**, and it became official again when the regime fell. And the detail that says
> the most: **modern performances typically omit the verse referencing the king.** A country singing
> its own history with one verse edited out.
>
> The composer, Mohamed Abdel Wahab, also wrote the music for **Tunisia's and the UAE's** anthems [2]
> — and is the same Egyptian composer credited on Egypt's list in some sources. One man's music sits
> under several of these borders.

**Framing notes.**

- **Omar Mukhtar is the entry point, not Gaddafi.** A trail that starts in 1969 tells a story about one
  man; a trail that starts in 1911 and passes through an executed teacher of the resistance tells the
  story of a country. Source [1] gives the execution date but no biography — that research is owed.
- **The name "Libya" was applied by Italy in 1934** [1]. Compare Burkina Faso, which *renamed itself*
  in 1984 out of its own languages ([`bf-burkina-faso.md`](bf-burkina-faso.md)). Two opposite
  directions of the same act, and worth telling together.
- **The ethnic figures are from 1999** [1] — a quarter of a century old and predating two civil wars.
  Cite the date whenever the numbers are used, or do not use them.

## Peoples & cultures

- **Arabs ~92%, Berbers ~5%, others ~3%** — source [1], **explicitly 1999 data**. Old, and to be
  labelled as old wherever it appears.
- **The Tuareg** — not in source [1], but present in the literature: Ibrahim al-Koni was raised in
  Tuareg tradition in the Fezzan [4], and the Tadrart Acacus rock art sits in the same desert [3].
- **Ghadamès, "the Pearl of the Desert"** — a Saharan oasis city with distinctive multi-storey
  architecture, still standing [3]. It is also al-Koni's birthplace [4]: heritage site and living
  literary origin in one place.

## Literature & voices

| Writer | Work | Year | Language | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ibrahim al-Koni (b. 1948, Ghadamis, Fezzan) | *Bleeding of the Stone* · *Anubis* · *The Seven Veils of Seth* · *Gold Dust* (the four in English translation) | `[NEEDS years]` | Arabic | [4] |
| Sadeq al-Neihum · Kamel Maghur · Muhammad al-Shaltami · Ali al-Regeie · Khalifa al-Fakhri | named as writers of the period; no works sourced | — | Arabic | [4] |

**Ibrahim al-Koni is the find in this file.** Source [4] records that he was **brought up in the
traditions of the Tuareg**, spent his childhood in the desert, and **learned to read and write Arabic
at twelve**; he studied at the Maxim Gorky Literature Institute in Moscow, worked as a journalist in
Moscow and Warsaw, and moved to Switzerland in 1993. He has been "hailed as a magical realist, a Sufi
fabulist, and a poetic novelist", and is described as one of the most prolific Arab novelists.

**The connection worth making.** A writer raised inside an oral desert tradition who came to written
Arabic at twelve and then turned that tradition into novels is doing precisely what this project
claims to be about: **carrying an oral inheritance into a written form without flattening it.** That
is Mutwa's problem and Vilakazi's problem. Al-Koni is the North African name to put beside them.

## Heritage & sites

Five UNESCO World Heritage Sites — **four of them listed as in danger** [3]:

| Site | Inscribed | Type | In danger | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leptis Magna | 1982 | Cultural | **Yes** | Founded as a Phoenician settlement; under Roman rule from 46 BCE |
| Sabratha | 1982 | Cultural | **Yes** | Phoenician trading post, later a Roman city; a theatre with three levels of columns |
| Cyrene | 1982 | Cultural | **Yes** | Greek colony established 631 BCE; damaged by earthquake and tsunami |
| Rock-Art Sites of Tadrart Acacus | 1985 | Cultural | **Yes** | Paintings and engravings spanning **12,000 BCE to 100 CE** |
| Old Town of Ghadamès | 1986 | Cultural | No — **removed from the endangered list in 2025** after restoration | Saharan oasis city, "the Pearl of the Desert" |

Three further sites are on the tentative list: Ghirza, Ptolemais and Haua Fteah Cave [3].

**Four in five in danger is the headline.** And **Ghadamès coming off the danger list in 2025 is the
counter-headline** — restoration worked. If Libya ever appears in the app, that pairing is the honest
one: not "a country losing its heritage", but a country that has already pulled one site back.
