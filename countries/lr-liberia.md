## 🔴 The Vai syllabary — and the 27 that followed

| | |
|---|---|
| **Invented by** | **Momolu Duwalu Bukele** of Jondu, in what is now Grand Cape Mount County [4] |
| **When** | First documented in **the 1830s**; thought by some to have been invented around **1833**, with claims as early as 1815 [4] |
| **What** | A **syllabary** — not an alphabet — devised for the **Vai language** [4] |
| **Origin story** | The syllabary is **said to have been revealed to Bukele in a dream**, and communicated to friends and tribal elders [4] |
| **Standing** | Bukele "is regarded within the Vai community and by most scholars as the syllabary's inventor and chief promoter" [4] |

**What happened next is the part that matters.** Source [4]:

- Bukele and his supporters **set up a school at Dshondu to teach it**, and more followed at
  Bandakoplo, Mala and elsewhere.
- Within a few generations it was used for **letters, engraving jewellery, drafting carpentry plans,
  keeping diaries and managing accounts**. The Vai **made their own ink from crushed berries**.
- **"The script was so successful that other Indigenous groups in the region were inspired to create
  their own; since the 1830s, at least 27 new scripts have been invented for West African
  languages."**

🔴 **That last sentence reframes this entire folder.** West Africa is not a region that *received*
literacy. It is a region that **invented writing systems repeatedly, for two centuries, from the
inside** — Vai in the 1830s, N'Ko in 1949 ([`gn-guinea.md`](gn-guinea.md)), and at least twenty-five
more. Ubuntu Heritage's premise is that African voices were silenced and must be reclaimed; the Vai
evidence sharpens it: **the voices were not only speaking, they were writing, in scripts they built
themselves, and that fact was left out of the story.**

⚠️ **One complication to keep, not hide.** Source [4] records that Tuchscherer and Hair (2002) present
evidence that **exposure to the Cherokee syllabary** — via part-Cherokee migrants from the US living
on the Liberian coast — was part of the process. This does not diminish Bukele's invention; it makes
it a documented case of **Indigenous-to-Indigenous transmission across the Atlantic**, which is more
interesting than either a pure-origin story or a derivative one. Tell it that way, with the caveat
that it is one scholarly account.

## National anthem

- **Title:** "All Hail, Liberia, Hail!" (English)
- **Adopted:** **lyrics 26 July 1847** (independence day) · **music composed 1860**
- **Words by:** **Daniel Bashiel Warner — Liberia's third president**
- **Music by:** Olmstead Luca
- **Recording we could use:** none sourced yet
- **Source:** [2]

> **The oldest anthem in this folder by more than a century** — its words date to 1847 [2]. Source [2]
> notes the melody dates only to 1860, "suggesting the anthem first had a different melody"; recorded
> here as the source's inference, not as fact.
>
> Source [2] describes the country as "founded by freed slaves from the United States in the 1820s" —
> see the framing note, because that sentence needs care.

**Framing notes.**

- ⚠️ **Liberia is the hardest country in this folder to narrate honestly, and the difficulty must not
  be smoothed away.** It was established as "a project of the American Colonization Society, which
  fomented the emigration of Black Americans from the United States to Africa" [1]. The settlers were
  formerly enslaved people returning to a continent their ancestors were taken from — **and they
  established a state over indigenous peoples who already lived there**, ruling as an
  **Americo-Liberian** class until **1980** [1]. Source [1]'s own word for the ACS's role is
  "fomented", and the ACS's motives in the United States were not straightforwardly liberationist.
  **Ubuntu Heritage must tell both halves: a return from slavery, and a settlement over others.**
  The project already refuses the sanitised version of Botswana's *botlhanka*
  ([`bw-botswana.md`](bw-botswana.md)) and Dahomey's role in the slave trade
  ([`bj-benin.md`](bj-benin.md)); this is the same obligation and it is sharper here.
- **Note the dates against each other.** Bukele's Vai script (c. 1833) is **contemporaneous with the
  ACS settlement (1822)** [1], [4]. While a colony was being founded on the coast in the name of
  bringing civilisation, a Vai man inland was inventing a writing system and opening schools to teach
  it. **Those two facts sit eleven years apart in the same country** and telling them together is the
  most powerful thing this file can do.
- **2005 and 2011 belong in the app.** Africa's first elected woman head of state, and a Nobel Peace
  Prize to a woman who organised for peace, in a country that had just come through two civil wars
  [1]. This project's canon puts *Mhudi*'s woman narrator at its centre; Liberia offers the
  contemporary counterpart.
- **The civil-war death toll (60,000–80,000 in the first war alone)** [1] is not atmosphere. Use it
  with its source or not at all.

## Peoples & cultures

- ⚠️ **No current demographic percentages are given by source [1]** — it names the historical
  indigenous groups (Dei, Bassa, Kru, Gola, Kissi, Gio, Mano, Grebo and others) without shares. **Do
  not invent them.**
- **The Americo-Liberian / indigenous distinction is the country's central social fact** until 1980
  [1], and it is a political category, not an ethnic one — handle as carefully as *ivoirité* in
  [`ci-cote-d-ivoire.md`](ci-cote-d-ivoire.md).
- **Vai literacy is a cultural practice, not just a script** — diaries, accounts, jewellery engraving,
  carpentry plans [4]. That is everyday written life in an African language, documented.
- **Mende, Kissi, Gola and Mandingo cross into Sierra Leone and Guinea** — see
  [`sl-sierra-leone.md`](sl-sierra-leone.md) and [`gn-guinea.md`](gn-guinea.md).

## Literature & voices

| Writer | Work | Year | Language | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Momolu Duwalu Bukele** | **the Vai syllabary** | c. **1833** | for Vai | [4] |
| **Bai T. Moore** (1916–1988) | ***Murder in the Cassava Patch*** — novella; "a crime of passion in a traditional Liberian setting" | **1968** | English | [4] |
| Bai T. Moore | collected **Vai folktales** and those of other ethnic groups, across the whole country | — | — | [4] |
| Daniel Bashiel Warner | the national anthem's words | 1847 | English | [2] |

**Bai T. Moore is the Liberian figure closest to what Ubuntu Heritage does.** Source [4]: a poet,
novelist, **folklorist** and essayist who held cultural and educational posts for the Liberian
government **and for UNESCO**, **founded Liberia's National Cultural Center**, and **travelled all
over Liberia collecting Vai folktales and those of other ethnic groups**. *Murder in the Cassava
Patch* (1968) "became such a classic in Liberian literature that it is still taught in high schools"
[4].

**That is precisely Credo Mutwa's role in this project's canon** — a collector and custodian of oral
tradition who also wrote — with the addition of a state cultural institution built around it. If
Ubuntu Heritage ever argues that oral-history collection is national infrastructure rather than
nostalgia, **Moore's National Cultural Center is the West African precedent.**
