**Official-language instrument:** `[NEEDS SOURCE]` — source [1] names both official languages but not
the constitutional article or its year. Tamazight's official status is recent and its instrument is
worth having precisely; compare Libya, where the article number *is* sourced
([`ly-libya.md`](ly-libya.md)).

**Languages Ubuntu Heritage does not have:** all of them.

**Why Morocco is the North African language story worth telling.** Standard Moroccan Tamazight is
**official**, and a quarter of the population speaks a Berber language, and yet the everyday language
of nine in ten people — Darija — is **neither**. Three tiers: the language of law, the language of
heritage, and the language people actually use. Ubuntu Heritage's own picker exists because South
Africa has a version of this problem; Morocco has a sharper one.

**Same structural blocker as Burkina Faso** — see [`bf-burkina-faso.md`](bf-burkina-faso.md#languages).

## National anthem

- **Title (own language):** النشيد الشريف — *an-Nashid ash-Sharif*; in French, *Hymne Chérifien*
- **Title (English):** "Hymn of the Sharif"
- **Adopted:** **1956** (music) · **1970** (lyrics)
- **Words by:** Ali Squalli Houssaini (علي الصقلي الحسيني)
- **Music by:** Léo Morgan
- **Recording we could use:** none sourced yet
- **Source:** [2]

> **The music is older than the independence it now celebrates.** Source [2] records that the melody
> was already in use **during the French protectorate, with different words**; at independence in 1956
> the tune was kept and new lyrics were written only **a decade and a half later, in 1970**. So
> Morocco's anthem is a colonial-era tune re-inhabited rather than replaced — the opposite choice to
> Libya's, which was deposed and restored ([`ly-libya.md`](ly-libya.md)). Neither is the "correct"
> way to decolonise a national song, and putting the two side by side says more than either alone.
>
> Source [2] also glosses *Sharif* as "one who is a protector of the tribe and all its assets".

**Framing notes.**

- ⚠️ **Western Sahara is disputed territory, and this file must not quietly take a side.** Source [1]'s
  own wording is that Morocco "occupies" it after the 1975 Green March. The sovereignty question is
  live at the UN and the African Union, and Morocco and the Sahrawi Polisario Front hold opposed
  positions. **Ubuntu Heritage should state that it is disputed and whose claims are in contest, and
  stop there.** Picking a formulation casually — including by drawing a map — would be the app
  asserting a position on a live conflict. This is the single most sensitive item in the North African
  files.
- **1777 is worth keeping** for the same reason Botswana's 1895 mission is: it is African diplomacy
  acting on the world, not being acted upon.
- **The Alawi dynasty has held power since 1631** [1] — nearly four centuries of continuity. A history
  trail that begins at colonisation would miss the point of this country entirely.

## Peoples & cultures

- **Arab and Amazigh (Berber)** — source [1] gives **no ethnic percentages at all**, only language
  shares (above). Given how politically charged Arab/Amazigh identity is across the Maghreb, the
  language figures are the safer ground and should be used instead of inventing ethnic ones.
- **Andalusian inheritance** — Tétouan's medina is inscribed for the influence of **Spanish refugees**
  [3]. The expulsions from Spain reshaped Moroccan cities; that is a migration story with a heritage
  site attached.
- **Jemaa el-Fnaa, Marrakesh** — named in the Marrakesh inscription [3]. It is one of the world's
  great living oral-performance spaces (storytellers, musicians) — `[NEEDS SOURCE]`, and if any single
  site on this continent belongs in a project about **oral tradition**, it is this square.

**The thread running through North Africa.** Source [4] records that **Choukri learned to read and
write only at twenty**, and that his autobiography is "one of the most powerful accounts of poverty
ever written in Arabic". Libya's Ibrahim al-Koni learned written Arabic at twelve
([`ly-libya.md`](ly-libya.md)). Two of the region's major novelists came to literacy late and wrote
anyway. **That is not a biographical curiosity — it is the same movement from spoken to written that
this entire project is about**, and it is the strongest cross-country pattern the research has turned
up so far.

**Fatema Mernissi belongs in the app's argument, not just its bibliography.** A sociologist writing
women's lives from inside them is the counterpart to what *Mhudi* does by making a woman the narrator
of the Difaqane. Note that Chraïbi's novels are also described as dealing with "the treatment of
women" [4]; the theme is not marginal in this canon.

## Heritage & sites

Nine UNESCO World Heritage Sites, all from source [3]:

| Site | Inscribed | Type | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medina of Fez | 1981 | Cultural | "One of the most extensive and best preserved old towns in the Muslim world" |
| Medina of Marrakesh | 1985 | Cultural | Founded in the 1070s; Koutoubia Mosque and Jemaa el-Fnaa |
| Ksar of Ait-Ben-Haddou | 1987 | Cultural | Fortified village on a trans-Saharan trade route; earthen buildings |
| Historic City of Meknes | 1996 | Cultural | 17th-century capital; monumental walls, Kasbah of Moulay Ismail |
| Volubilis | 1997 | Cultural | Founded 3rd century BCE; Roman mosaics |
| Medina of Tétouan | 1997 | Cultural | Andalusian influence from Spanish refugees |
| Medina of Essaouira | 2001 | Cultural | 18th-century port; Vauban military engineering principles |
| Portuguese City of Mazagan (El Jadida) | 2004 | Cultural | 16th-century Portuguese fortress, Renaissance star fort |
| Rabat | 2012 | Cultural | Early 20th-century capital redesign around the 12th-century Kasbah of the Udayas and Hassan Tower |

**Ait-Ben-Haddou is the trans-Saharan trade made visible** — the same network that runs through
Burkina Faso's Loropéni ([`bf-burkina-faso.md`](bf-burkina-faso.md)) and Mali's cities. These files
should eventually be linked to each other along that road rather than treated as nine separate
countries.
