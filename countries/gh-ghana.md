**Languages Ubuntu Heritage does not have:** the ten government-sponsored languages, and the others.

**Ghana is structurally the closest country in this folder to South Africa's own arrangement.** One
official language plus **ten formally sponsored indigenous languages** [1] is the same shape as
South Africa's Constitution §6 — a state that names many languages rather than one. Ubuntu Heritage's
whole language layer exists to serve exactly that shape.

That makes `gh` the **strongest candidate for the next `country-languages.ts` entry after the Southern
African six** — with the Zimbabwe-style honesty: `lead: "en"` because it is the only language the app
speaks there, and a `notYet` list naming all ten. The entry would then say the true thing: *Ghana
sponsors ten languages and we have none of them.*

## National anthem

- **Title:** "God Bless Our Homeland Ghana" (English)
- **Adopted:** **1957** (music) · **1966** (current lyrics)
- **Words by:** unknown — chosen by public contest [2]
- **Music by:** Philip Gbeho
- **Recording we could use:** none sourced yet
- **Source:** [2]

> **The lyrics changed when the government fell.** Source [2]: Gbeho composed the music for
> independence in 1957; the original words were replaced in **1966**, after Nkrumah's government was
> overthrown, and **the current lyrics came out of a public contest** — which is why the lyricist is
> recorded as unknown.
>
> Three West African anthems in this folder have now been altered for political reasons — Ghana's
> words after a coup, Libya's restored after a revolution ([`ly-libya.md`](ly-libya.md)), Tunisia's
> reinstated after a deposition ([`tn-tunisia.md`](tn-tunisia.md)). **A national anthem is not a fixed
> object**, and any app feature that presents one as timeless will be saying something false.

**Framing notes.**

- ⚠️ **The forts are slave forts.** Source [3] calls them "fortified trading-posts"; Elmina and Cape
  Coast were holding sites in the transatlantic trade. **Ubuntu Heritage must not repeat UNESCO's
  euphemism.** These are among the most visited sites of return for the African diaspora, and what
  they were is the reason. `[NEEDS SOURCE]` for the specifics — and it must be a good one.
- ⚠️ **The War of the Golden Stool was led by a woman.** Source [1] gives the war and the defeat but
  not **Yaa Asantewaa**, who by widespread account led the Asante against the British in 1900.
  `[NEEDS SOURCE]` — do not state it until sourced, but do not leave the gap unmarked either: this is
  potentially the most important omission in the file, and a direct counterpart to *Mhudi*'s
  insistence on a woman at the centre of a war story.
- **The Golden Stool itself is the point of that war** — a sacred object embodying the Asante nation,
  which the British governor demanded to sit on. `[NEEDS SOURCE]`. If it holds, it is a near-perfect
  illustration of what colonial powers failed to understand about African political objects.
- **1957 begins the wave.** Nkrumah's Ghana is the reference point for almost every independence date
  elsewhere in this folder, and his pan-Africanism is the intellectual bridge to Sankara
  ([`bf-burkina-faso.md`](bf-burkina-faso.md)) and Cabral ([`cv-cabo-verde.md`](cv-cabo-verde.md)).

- **The Mossi origin story starts here.** [`bf-burkina-faso.md`](bf-burkina-faso.md) records that the
  Mossi trace their founding to Princess Yennenga's flight from the **Mamprusi kingdom of modern-day
  northern Ghana**. Ghana's **Mole-Dagbon** (18.5%) and **Gurunsi** (2.7%) [1] are the peoples of that
  same northern region, and Gurunsi appears in Burkina Faso's table too. **Two files, one story, split
  by a colonial border.**
- ⚠️ **Kente and adinkra are not mentioned by source [1] at all** — a striking absence, since they are
  among the most recognisable African visual systems in the world. **Adinkra symbols are a writing
  system of sorts**, each carrying a proverb. `[NEEDS SOURCE]`, and worth real effort: a project about
  African voices that reaches Ghana and does not deal with adinkra has missed the obvious thing.
- **Ananse the spider** — the trickster of Akan oral tradition, and the basis of Efua Sutherland's
  *The Marriage of Anansewa* [4]. Ananse crossed the Atlantic and became Anansi in the Caribbean.
  `[NEEDS SOURCE]` for the crossing, but the play is sourced and it is the door.

**This is the best-dated literature section in the folder** — thirteen works with years.

**Three connections worth making, and they are strong.**

1. **Efua Sutherland's *The Marriage of Anansewa* (1975) does exactly what Ubuntu Heritage claims to
   do**: it takes **Ananse**, a figure from Akan oral storytelling, and builds a modern stage play out
   of him — the oral form carried into a written one *on purpose*, by a named author, with the
   tradition still legible inside it. That is the project's method, executed fifty years ago.
2. **Ama Ata Aidoo's *The Dilemma of a Ghost* (1965)** — an African writer, in the first decade of
   independence, writing the encounter between the continent and its diaspora. Ghana's coastal forts
   are where that separation physically happened. The literature and the heritage sites are the same
   subject.
3. **Kofi Awoonor** drew on Ewe oral poetry — the *dirge* tradition — `[NEEDS SOURCE]`, but if it
   holds he is the West African counterpart to **Vilakazi**: a university poet writing modern verse
   out of an indigenous oral form. Awoonor was killed in the 2013 Westgate attack in Nairobi
   `[NEEDS SOURCE]`; verify before use, and handle with care.

## Heritage & sites

Two UNESCO World Heritage Sites [3]:

| Site | Inscribed | Type | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Forts and Castles** — Volta, Greater Accra, Central and Western Regions | 1979 | Cultural | "The remains of fortified trading-posts, **erected between 1482 and 1786**", along the coast |
| **Asante Traditional Buildings** | 1980 | Cultural | "The last material remains of the great Asante civilization, which reached its high point in the 18th century" |

- ⚠️ **See the framing note above on what the forts were.** UNESCO's phrase "fortified trading-posts"
  is doing a great deal of work.
- **"The last material remains"** [3] is a hard sentence. The Asante built in materials that do not
  survive, and the British burned Kumasi `[NEEDS SOURCE]`. What is inscribed is what was left after a
  war — which is a fact about the inscription, not only about the buildings.
